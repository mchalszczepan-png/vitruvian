# Cross-country evidence: diagnosis waits, stage, GP behaviour (Finland lens)

Date: 2026-11-14. Research only, no code.

Tags: **READ** = I read the page or abstract. **SNIPPET** = search summary only. **UNVERIFIED** = memory or secondary, not checked. **[source needed]** = claim kept, no URL found.

Source note: DOI links below are doi.org links for the DOIs named in the file. Each DOI returned a redirect to its publisher when checked. Full text was read only where the row says READ.

Acronyms (once): ICBP = International Cancer Benchmarking Partnership (research group comparing countries). OECD = Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. EU = European Union. ECIR = European Cancer Inequalities Registry. ECA = European Court of Auditors. iPAAC = EU Joint Action on innovative cancer partnership. ENCR = European Network of Cancer Registries. JRC = EU Joint Research Centre. ECIS = European Cancer Information System. CPP = Cancer Patient Pathway (Denmark's fast route). GP = family doctor. PCP = primary care practitioner. SES = socio-economic status. OR = odds ratio (above 1 = higher odds). RS = relative survival. PBCR = population-based cancer registry. DOI = permanent paper ID.

Problem numbers: P1 = nobody measures total wait. P3 = no fast lane from public GP. P4 = stage missing for ~58% of Finnish cases [source needed]. Lens = two-speed entry by income.

**Important:** Finland is not in the ICBP wait-interval studies (Weller 2018, Rose 2015, Walters 2013, Eden 2019). Finland's own data is in the OECD profile only.

## OECD (Finland and comparisons)

- **Finland 2025 profile (row 14), READ.** Waiting-time targets: six weeks from suspected-cancer referral to start of primary treatment; four weeks from surgery to adjuvant therapy (same [PDF](https://cancer-inequalities.jrc.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/docs/ccp2025/ec-oecd-fi-2024-1666-en.pdf), section on care). Cancer mortality gap by education: men 62% (EU 84%), women 43% (EU 37%) (same PDF). Breast screening participation: 66% in lower education vs 85% in higher (same PDF, breast screening section). Five-year survival by region does not differ much, which the report reads as similar quality of care across the country (same PDF). The Finnish Cancer Registry is monitored regularly and considered complete and up to date (Seppä et al., 2023) (corrected: the earlier draft said "audited"). Finland plans to expand the cancer quality registry to report stage at diagnosis, waiting times, treatment and outcomes (same PDF). **This is the strongest Finland-specific point for P1 and P4.**
- **Beating Cancer Inequalities 2024 (row 15), READ highlights.** Countries that monitor waits: Denmark, Iceland, and Finland (fines) [source needed for the fines and monitoring claims; see [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1787/14fdc89a-en)]. Denmark: if a region cannot treat within the maximum wait, the patient must be sent elsewhere in the country or abroad [source needed]. Several countries collect patient-reported measures [source needed]. Monitoring of cancer care quality with feedback to providers in Estonia, France, Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia [source needed].
- **Not verified:** whether the 2024 report gives a Finland-vs-others wait comparison in numbers.

## EU Joint Actions and registry work

- **iPAAC (SNIPPET).** Its registry work package is Italian-led. It aims to link population cancer registries with clinical and administrative data to build care-pathway indicators. Deliverable: a roadmap on implementing cancer control actions ([ipaac.eu/about](https://ipaac.eu/about), SNIPPET; encr.eu news [source needed]). No specific stage or waiting-time target found in what I read.
- **ENCR and JRC (SNIPPET).** Data protocol for ECIS uses stage codes (TNM where available). Quality-check rules for stage and treatment fields exist (encr.eu PDFs [source needed]). - **[Bosetti 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.70593) (row 13, READ landing page).** Reports stage completeness for six cancers. Ongoing gaps in some countries.

## Correlation vs proven

| Claim | Type of evidence | Proven? |
|---|---|---|
| Shorter waits give better survival | Mixed observational studies ([Neal 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/bjc.2015.48)) | No. Plausible for some cancers only. |
| GP readiness to investigate goes with better survival | Ecological, 11 jurisdictions ([Rose 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2014-007212)) | No. Correlation. Baltic counterexample ([Rosendahl 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02813432.2025.2451653)). |
| GP gatekeeping goes with lower survival | Ecological, 19 countries ([Vedsted and Olesen 2011](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp11x588484)) | No. Confounding likely. |
| Denmark CPP fast route goes with better survival | Before/after cohort ([Jensen 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-017-3623-8)) | No. Lead-time and selection bias possible. |
| Stage explains part of survival gaps | Registry comparison ([Walters 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/thoraxjnl-2012-202297)) | Partly. Stage-specific gaps remain. |
| Stage data gaps distort comparisons | Registry rule tests ([Eden 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.canep.2018.10.019)) | Yes for registry rules (adjustment changed rankings). |

## What this means for Finland

- **P1 (measure the total wait):** ICBP has a working method (patient, GP, diagnostic and treatment intervals from registry plus questionnaires) ([Weller 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-023870)). Finland is missing from these studies, so a Finnish version would be new evidence. OECD says Finland plans to add waiting times to its registry, which is the cheapest route to the total wait (see [OECD profile](https://cancer-inequalities.jrc.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/docs/ccp2025/ec-oecd-fi-2024-1666-en.pdf)).
- **P3 (fast lane for suspected cancer):** Denmark's CPP is the closest real example. Its evidence is observational ([Jensen 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-017-3623-8)). The GP-readiness link ([Rose 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2014-007212)) is correlation only, and the Baltic data ([Rosendahl 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02813432.2025.2451653)) show GP action alone does not settle survival. [Harris 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-022904) suggests direct specialist access and fast-track systems matter.
- **P4 (missing stage):** Stage completeness is a known weak point across registries ([Bosetti 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.70593)). [Eden 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.canep.2018.10.019) shows that registry rules can move survival by up to 7 points, so Finland's missing stage affects comparisons. Finland's own plan to add stage to its quality registry is the lever (see OECD profile above). The 58% Finnish stage-missing figure itself is [source needed] (the repo's own stage-data notes mark it unverified).
- **Lens (two-speed entry):** Finland's OECD profile gives the clearest income-type gap: 5-year survival 13 points lower for lower-education people, and lower screening participation (see OECD profile above). Explain this as a correlation with access, not proof of cause.
- **Next step for the team:** use Denmark and Norway as benchmarks for fast-lane design. Treat [Rose 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2014-007212) and [Vedsted 2011](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp11x588484) as hypotheses, not evidence of effect.
